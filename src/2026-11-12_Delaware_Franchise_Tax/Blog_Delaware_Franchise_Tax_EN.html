Many founders of Delaware corporations open their first franchise tax notice and see a number like $85,165. For a young startup with a few hundred thousand dollars in the bank, that looks impossible. In most of these cases, the number is not what the company actually has to pay. It is simply the tax under one of two methods Delaware allows, and the other method can be a few hundred dollars.

This post shows you how to figure both methods yourself, with formulas you can type into Excel or Google Sheets, so you can check your 2026 annual report before the March 1, 2027 deadline. It applies to Delaware corporations. Delaware LLCs, LPs and GPs pay a flat annual tax instead and are covered briefly at the end.

The rule in one sentence

Delaware's Division of Corporations says the tax can be figured with the Authorized Shares method or the Assumed Par Value Capital method, and you should use the method that results in the lesser tax. The first method looks only at how many shares your charter authorizes. The second also looks at how many shares you actually issued and how much the company owns (its total gross assets).

Step 1: Gather four numbers

  1. Authorized shares and par value per share, from your certificate of incorporation (including every amendment). If you have more than one class, such as common and preferred, note each class separately.
  2. Issued shares at year-end, including treasury shares, from your stock ledger or cap table.
  3. Total gross assets: the "total assets" figure on Form 1120, Schedule L, for the fiscal year ending in 2026.

Step 2: Figure the Authorized Shares method

The schedule on Delaware's "How to Calculate Franchise Taxes" page is:

Authorized sharesTax
5,000 or fewer$175 (minimum)
5,001 to 10,000$250
Each additional 10,000 shares or portionadd $85
Maximum$200,000

Put authorized shares in cell B1 and type this formula:

=MIN(IF(B1<=5000,175,IF(B1<=10000,250,250+CEILING((B1-10000)/10000,1)*85)),200000)

For 10,000,000 authorized shares: 9,990,000 shares above the first 10,000 make 999 blocks. 999 × $85 = $84,915, plus $250 = $85,165. That is where the scary number comes from.

Step 3: Figure the Assumed Par Value Capital method

  1. Assumed par = total gross assets ÷ issued shares, to 6 decimal places.
  2. For each class whose par value is below assumed par, multiply assumed par by that class's authorized shares. For a class whose par value is above assumed par, multiply its own par value by its authorized shares.
  3. Add the results. This is the assumed par value capital.
  4. The tax is $400 for each $1,000,000 or portion, so round up to the next million. The minimum is $400 and the maximum is $200,000.

With gross assets in B2, issued shares in B3 and par value in B4 (one class), these formulas do it:

B5 (assumed par): =ROUND(B2/B3,6)
B6 (capital): =IF(B4<B5,B5,B4)*B1
B7 (tax): =MIN(MAX(400,CEILING(B6/1000000,1)*400),200000)

Then the tax you pay is the lower one: =MIN(B7, Authorized Shares result). The annual report filing fee of $50 for a non-exempt domestic corporation is added on top.

In the Series A case, assumed par is $8,000,000 ÷ 12,000,000 = $0.666667. Both classes have a lower par, so assumed par applies to all 20,000,000 authorized shares: $13,333,340, which rounds up to 14 millions × $400 = $5,600. In the high-par case, the $1.00 par is above the $0.50 assumed par, so the par value is used. In the small no-par company, the Authorized Shares minimum of $175 wins; Delaware notes that for no-par stock this method always gives the lesser tax.

Step 4: File, pay and plan ahead

  • March 1, 2027 (Monday): the 2026 annual report and franchise tax are due, filed online. Enter both issued shares and total gross assets in the report; the Assumed Par Value Capital method depends on those two figures.
  • Tax of $5,000 or more: Delaware asks for estimated payments: 40% by June 1, 20% by September 1, 20% by December 1, and the rest by March 1.
  • Late: a $200 penalty plus 1.5% interest per month on the tax and penalty.
  • Shares changed during the year? Delaware prorates each period by the days it was in effect over 365 and adds the periods together.

Common mistakes

  1. Paying the first number you see without figuring the second method.
  2. Dividing by authorized shares instead of issued shares when you figure assumed par.
  3. Leaving out treasury shares or a second class of stock.
  4. Using a different asset figure (cash only, or equity) instead of total assets on Schedule L.
  5. Forgetting the $50 filing fee, which makes the payment short.
  6. Paying a look-alike notice. Delaware warns businesses to be suspicious of mail that does not come from the State or the company's registered agent.

FAQ

Is choosing the Assumed Par Value Capital method a special election?

Delaware's instructions simply say to use the method that results in the lesser tax. In practice, you enter issued shares and gross assets in the online annual report so the second method can be applied.

My company had almost no assets. Do I still owe tax?

Yes. Each method has a minimum: $175 under Authorized Shares and $400 under Assumed Par Value Capital, plus the $50 report fee. With very few assets, watch the par value: if it is higher than assumed par, the par value is used.

Does this apply to my Delaware LLC?

No. Delaware LLCs, LPs and GPs file no franchise tax report. They pay a flat annual tax by June 1; the Division of Corporations page reviewed in November 2026 lists $400.

What if the online system shows a different number from my spreadsheet?

Check the figures you entered against your stock ledger and Form 1120 first. If they match and the amount still differs, contact the Division of Corporations or your registered agent.

Checklist

  • Authorized shares and par value for each class, from the latest charter
  • Issued shares at year-end, including treasury shares
  • Total assets from Form 1120, Schedule L
  • Both methods figured; lower result noted
  • Report filed and tax + $50 paid by March 1, 2027
  • Estimate dates on the calendar if the tax is $5,000 or more
