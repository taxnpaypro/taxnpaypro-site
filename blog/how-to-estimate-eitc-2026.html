How to Estimate Your 2026 Earned Income Tax Credit (Even If You Are Self-Employed)

↓ Get the free Excel + PDF kit for this article

Every January, many workers, families and small business owners ask the same question: "Will I get the Earned Income Tax Credit this year, and about how much?" The EITC is a refundable federal credit for people who work and have low to moderate income, and it can be worth several thousand dollars for a family with children. It also changes every year, and it works differently from most credits: it first grows as you earn more, then levels off, then shrinks. If you are self-employed, there is one more twist, because your business profit counts as earned income.

This guide shows how to estimate the credit for tax year 2026 (the return you file in 2027) with a calculator or a simple spreadsheet, using the official IRS figures.

How the credit is shaped

The EITC moves in three stages. In the phase-in stage, each extra dollar of earned income raises the credit. Once earned income reaches the "earned income amount", you are on the plateau and get the maximum credit. After income passes the phase-out start, the credit phases out until it reaches $0 at the income limit.

Step by step: estimate it yourself

  1. Check the basic rules first. Use the flowchart below. If any answer is "No", the credit is generally not available, or only the no-child credit may apply.
  2. Add up your earned income. Taxable wages, salaries and tips (W-2 box 1) plus net earnings from self-employment. The IRS says net earnings from self-employment are included in earned income for the EITC. If you are self-employed, the exact amount is worked out on EIC Worksheet B in the Form 1040 instructions. A business loss can lower the total; follow EIC Worksheet B.
  3. Find your row in the table. Pick the column for your number of qualifying children and the rows for your filing status.
  4. Work out two rates. Phase-in rate = maximum credit ÷ earned income amount. Phase-out rate = maximum credit ÷ (income limit − phase-out start). For one child in 2026 these come to 34% and about 15.98%.
  5. Phase-in credit = the smaller of (earned income × phase-in rate) and the maximum credit.
  6. Reduction = phase-out rate × (the larger of earned income and AGI − phase-out start). If the result is negative, use 0.
  7. Estimate = the smaller of the phase-in credit and (maximum credit − reduction), but never below $0. If your income is at or above the limit, or investment income is over $12,200, the estimate is $0.

Why the larger of earned income and AGI? IRS Publication 596 shows this in its worked example: when AGI is different from earned income, the IRS worksheet looks up both amounts and the smaller credit applies.

In Excel or Google Sheets, put earned income in B1, AGI in B2, the earned income amount in B3, the maximum credit in B4, the phase-out start in B5 and the income limit in B6, then type:

=IF(MAX(B1,B2)>=B6,0,MAX(0,ROUND(MIN(B1*B4/B3,B4-B4/(B6-B5)*MAX(0,MAX(B1,B2)-B5)),0)))

Two edge cases are worth testing. If example A also had $13,000 of investment income, the estimate would be $0 because it is over the $12,200 limit. If example A had wages of $20,000 but AGI of $30,000 (because of other income such as a pension), the phase-out would use the $30,000 and the estimate would drop to about $3,451. The "smaller of" rule matters most when earned income is low but AGI is higher: with 1 child, earned income of $5,000 and AGI of $30,000, the phase-in credit is $1,700 and the maximum minus the reduction is about $3,451, so the estimate is the smaller amount, $1,700.

Notes for self-employed owners

  • The IRS says a self-employed person must report all business income and deduct all allowable business expenses. Leaving out either one to change the credit is not allowed.
  • The profit and loss report from your accounting software is a starting point. The earned income figure for the EITC comes from EIC Worksheet B in the Form 1040 instructions.
  • Keep business records, receipts and bank statements that support the income and expenses you report.

Common mistakes

  1. Counting a child who does not meet the age, relationship or residency test, or who has no valid SSN by the return due date.
  2. Two people claiming the same child.
  3. Using gross sales instead of net self-employment earnings.
  4. Forgetting investment income such as interest, dividends, capital gains and rents.
  5. Using last year's limits. The 2025 maximum for three or more children was $8,046; for 2026 it is $8,231.

FAQ

Can I get the EITC with an ITIN?

IRS Publication 596 says you cannot get the credit if you (or your spouse, on a joint return) have an ITIN instead of an SSN. The SSN must be issued by the due date of the return, including extensions.

My child has no SSN. Can I still claim something?

According to the IRS, a child without a valid SSN does not count as a qualifying child for the EITC, but you may still qualify for the smaller credit for workers without a qualifying child if you meet those rules, including the age 25 to 64 test.

Is there anything new on the 2026 return?

The IRS says that beginning with tax year 2026, you may have to complete Schedule 3-A, Federal Public Benefit, if you claim the EITC. Check the Form 1040 instructions when you file.

What happens if a claim is denied?

IRS Publication 596 explains that if the credit was denied because of reckless or intentional disregard of the rules, you cannot claim it for the next 2 years, and for fraud, the next 10 years. After a denial you may need to attach Form 8862 to claim it again.

Checklist

  • ☐ Valid SSN for you, your spouse and each child you count, by the return due date
  • ☐ No child: you lived in the US more than half of 2026 (with a child: the child lived with you in the US more than half the year)
  • ☐ Investment income $12,200 or less
  • ☐ Earned income added up (wages + net self-employment earnings)
  • ☐ AGI checked against the income limit for your filing status and children
  • ☐ Estimate worked out with the 2026 figures, not last year's
  • ☐ Records kept for income, expenses and where each child lived
  • ☐ Result confirmed with the IRS EITC Assistant or a licensed tax professional
