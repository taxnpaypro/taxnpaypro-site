How to Calculate Your Federal Income Tax by Bracket (2026 and 2027): Marginal vs. Effective Rate, Step by Step

↓ Get the free Excel + PDF kit for this article

"We're in the 22% bracket, so a bonus will just get eaten by taxes." Small business owners and employees say this every December, right when they are deciding on year-end bonuses, raises, invoices and retirement contributions. The sentence mixes up two different numbers: the marginal rate (the rate on your next dollar) and the effective rate (what you pay on average). If you can calculate both yourself, year-end decisions get much easier. This guide shows the method with the 2026 and 2027 IRS tables, worked examples and formulas you can type into Excel or Google Sheets.

This article is general educational information, not tax or legal advice.

Step 1: Start from taxable income, not gross pay

The brackets apply to taxable income: total income, minus adjustments (for example half of self-employment tax, deductible IRA or HSA contributions), minus the standard deduction or your itemized deductions (whichever is larger), minus any other deductions you qualify for, such as the qualified business income deduction.

The 2026 standard deduction is $16,100 for single filers and married filing separately, $32,200 for married filing jointly and $24,150 for head of household. For 2027 the IRS announced $16,600, $33,200 and $24,925. If you are 65 or older or blind, an extra standard deduction applies (2026: $2,050 if unmarried, $1,650 per married person).

Step 2: Find the right table

Each bracket "starts" at a taxable-income line. Every dollar above a line is taxed at that line's rate until the next line. Here are the official starting points.

Step 3: Tax each layer and add them up

Picture your taxable income as a stack of layers. The bottom layer is taxed at 10%, the next at 12%, and so on. Crossing a line never changes the tax on the dollars below it.

Worked example A (example values): single, 2026, taxable income $60,000

  1. 10% on the first $12,400 = $1,240
  2. 12% on $12,400 to $50,400 ($38,000) = $4,560
  3. 22% on $50,400 to $60,000 ($9,600) = $2,112
  4. Total federal income tax = $7,912

Shortcut: take the tax at the start of your bracket (the "base tax", $5,800 at $50,400) and add your rate times the amount over the line: $5,800 + 22% × $9,600 = $7,912.

Marginal rate = 22% (the rate on the next dollar).
Effective rate = $7,912 ÷ $60,000 = 13.19%.
Room left in the 22% bracket = $105,700 − $60,000 = $45,700.

Step 4: Do it in Excel or Google Sheets

Put the seven starting lines for your year and status in B2:H2 (for single 2026: 0, 12400, 50400, 105700, 201775, 256225, 640600). In B3:H3 put each rate's step-up over the rate below it: 10%, 2%, 10%, 2%, 8%, 3%, 2%. Type taxable income in A1. Then:

Tax: =SUMPRODUCT((A1>B2:H2)*(A1-B2:H2)*B3:H3) Marginal rate: =INDEX({0.10,0.12,0.22,0.24,0.32,0.35,0.37},MATCH(A1,B2:H2,1)) Effective rate: =IF(A1>0,tax_cell/A1,0)

The first formula works because every dollar above a line picks up the extra percentage of that bracket. Check it against example A: it returns $7,912.

Step 5: Test a raise, a bonus or a deduction

To see what a change really does, calculate the tax twice (before and after) and subtract. Three examples, all with example values:

Example A: the raise is taxed at 22%, so 78 cents of each new dollar stays with the employee (before payroll and state taxes). Example B: the deduction straddles two brackets. $8,600 comes off the 24% layer ($2,064) and $1,400 off the 22% layer ($308), so it saves $2,372, less than 24% × $10,000. Example C: the whole bonus sits in the 22% layer, so it adds $660 of federal income tax.

Step 6: Compare 2026 and 2027 before December 31

If you expect lower taxable income next year, moving income into 2027 or pulling a deduction into 2026 can lower the two-year total. Work it out by computing both years' tax for "no move" and "move".

In this example the move saves about $172, because $8,600 of the moved amount leaves a 24% layer in 2026 and lands in a 22% layer in 2027. If both years were in the same bracket, the move would change almost nothing. Remember that cash-basis income is generally taxed when you receive it or it is made available to you, so a check already in hand in December belongs to December.

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying all income by the top bracket rate.
  • Using gross pay instead of taxable income.
  • Believing a raise can reduce take-home pay because of brackets. It cannot; only the dollars above the line pay the higher rate.
  • Valuing a deduction at your top rate when it crosses into the bracket below.
  • Using last year's table. The lines move with inflation every year (the same $60,000 single income owes $7,741.50 under the 2027 table).
  • Mixing in long-term capital gains or qualified dividends, which use 0%, 15% and 20% rates.

FAQ

Does moving into a higher bracket raise the tax on all my income?

No. Only the dollars above the bracket line are taxed at the higher rate.

Which rate should I use to estimate the value of a deduction?

Start with your marginal rate, then check whether the deduction pushes you below a bracket line. If it does, part of it is worth the lower rate, as in example B.

Is my effective rate the same as the percentage withheld from my paycheck?

Not necessarily. Withholding is an estimate, and this calculation covers federal income tax only, not Social Security, Medicare or state tax.

Does this include self-employment tax?

No. Self-employment tax is a separate calculation. Half of it is an adjustment that lowers taxable income in step 1.

Quick checklist

  • ☐ Taxable income worked out (or copied from last year's return as a starting point)
  • ☐ Right year and filing status
  • ☐ Tax calculated layer by layer and checked with the base-tax shortcut
  • ☐ Marginal and effective rate noted
  • ☐ Raise, bonus or deduction tested before and after
  • ☐ 2026 vs. 2027 timing compared before December 31
